# The PGL(2,7) eight-card orbit scheme

## The objects

- **Cards** = `'I_8`, identified with the projective line P¹(F₇): codes 0–6 are field points, code 7 is the point at infinity (`pgl27_group.v`).
- **Deck** = an arrangement `sh : 8.-tuple 'I_8` with all cards distinct (`deck_ok`). Position i holds card `tnth sh i`.
- **Secret** = one bit. It is the PGL(2,7) **orbit class of the four "heart" positions** (hearts are cards with code < 4): compute the cross-ratio of the heart four-subset; class = `true` iff it is *equianharmonic* (cross-ratio in {3,5}), else *harmonic* (`orbit_class` in `pgl27_orbit.v`). Of the 70 four-subsets, 28 are equianharmonic and 42 harmonic (`orbit_class_split`), so both classes are populated.
- **Shuffle group** = the group generated by three permutations of the 8 cards: translation z↦z+1, scaling z↦3z, inversion z↦−1/z. This is exactly PGL(2,7), 336 elements (`pgl27_card`), acting sharply 3-transitively.

## The roles (ten processes, `pgl27_run.v`)

- **Dealer** (process 0): knows the secret bit `s`. Encodes it as a valid deck `orbit_encode s`, draws a shuffle `w0`, pre-applies the permutation `rho(w0)` to the deal, hands one card to each seat. The player never computes anything; the dealer does all the group action up front.
- **8 Players** (processes 2–9, seats `'I_8`): each player is a pure lookup: receive the card at position `rho(w0)(start_i)`, hold it, then reveal it in the public reveal phase.
- **Verifier** (process 1): collects the eight revealed endpoints and runs reconstruction.

## The steps

1. Dealer encodes: `ts_encode orbit_scheme s = orbit_encode s`, a distinct deck whose heart four-set has class `s`.
2. Dealer shuffles: `w0` uniform over the 336 group elements (ideal model), or a random 200-letter word over the five symmetrized generators (realistic model, `pgl27_mixing.v`).
3. Deal: seat i gets the card at `rho(w0)` applied to its start position.
4. Reveal: all eight players publicly reveal; the verifier collects the endpoints (`pgl27_endpoints`).
5. Reconstruct: verifier applies `ts_recon orbit_scheme = orbit_class` to the 8-card arrangement (find the hearts, take the cross-ratio, test equianharmonic) and outputs `s`.

## The record being instantiated (`ThresholdScheme bool 'I_8`, `pgg_sharing_framework.v:47`)

- `ts_T' = 7`: eight shares (one per card position).
- `ts_k' = 3`: privacy parameter; the field `ts_private` demands every coalition with `#|C| < 4` learn nothing.
- `ts_valid = orbit_valid`: "sh is a distinct deck of class s".
- `ts_recon = orbit_class`, `ts_encode = orbit_encode`.
- Proof obligations filled by `orbit_correct`, `pgl27_private`, `orbit_encode_valid` (`pgl27_scheme.v:81`).
- `pgl27_plug` then packages the scheme as a `ReconPlug` over the monodromy `pgl27_M`, with reconstruction invariant under any shuffle (`orbit_recon_invariant`), which is exactly why step 5 works for every cut `w0`.

## What "security threshold" means here

This is a **ramp scheme**, not a perfect (k, n) threshold scheme. The ladder:

- **≤ 3 positions: perfectly private.** Any 3-card coalition view can be re-dealt to *either* secret (`pgl27_private`); probabilistically, the view is independent of the secret (`pgl27_view_indep`).
- **4 positions: leaks.** The threshold is sharp: a 4-card view is dependent on the secret with strictly positive mutual information (`pgl27_view_dep_k4`, `pgl27_view_leak_k4`). Exact collision counts 96/72/36/12 at reveal sizes 4–6 are in `pgl27_leakage_census.v`.
- **≤ 6 positions: still ambiguous.** Six reveals never determine the class: two valid decks of opposite class agree on any six positions (`pgl27_six_reveal_ambiguous`).
- **7 positions: determined.** Distinctness leaves one missing card, so seven reveals fix the deck and hence the class (`pgl27_seven_reveal_class`).
- **8 positions: what the implemented decoder actually reads.**

So: private up to 3, leaky from 4, ambiguous through 6, determined at 7.

## Theorem map, re-keyed (mechanism first, lemma name second)

- **The engine (`pgl27_3transitive`) — key: the shuffle group can move any 3 cards anywhere.** PGL(2,7) is *sharply* 3-transitive on the 8 points: there are 8·7·6 = 336 ordered triples and exactly 336 group elements, one per triple. This one fact plays the role Lagrange interpolation plays for Shamir: everything below is it, dressed up.

- **Correctness (`pgl27_run_recovers`) — key: the secret is a group invariant, so shuffling can't change it.** The secret is the cross-ratio class of the heart positions, and cross-ratio is exactly the thing Möbius maps preserve. Whatever shuffle the dealer applies, the verifier reads off the same class that was dealt. (Shamir's analogue: f(0) survives because interpolation recovers f exactly.)

- **Privacy at ≤ 3 (`pgl27_private`, `pgl27_view_indep`) — key: 3 cards can be re-dealt to either secret.** Given any 3-card view under secret s₁, 3-transitivity provides a shuffle carrying some deck of class s₂ onto a deck showing those same 3 cards at those same positions. So both secrets explain every ≤3-card view, and under the uniform shuffle they explain it equally often: H(S | any ≤3 cards) = H(S). This is literally the same statement shape as Shamir's privacy, with "one interpolating polynomial per secret" replaced by "equally many shuffles per secret."

- **Sharpness at 4 (`pgl27_view_dep_k4`) — key: 4 points is where the invariant lives.** The group is only 3-transitive because 4 points carry an invariant, the cross-ratio. So a 4-card coalition is holding a sample of the very quantity that defines the secret; its view must correlate with it. The leak at exactly 4 and the existence of the secret have the same cause. (The census file then puts numbers on it: of the 336 shuffles, 96 produce colliding views at the representative 4-set.)

- **Ambiguity through 6 (`pgl27_six_reveal_ambiguous`) — key: the two unseen cards can be arranged to give either class.** For any 2 hidden positions there exist two valid decks, one per class, agreeing on all 6 visible cards. A concrete witness pair is built once, and 2-transitivity transports it to every choice of hidden pair. So 6 cards *leak* (from the previous item) but never *determine*.

- **Recovery at 7 (`pgl27_seven_reveal_class`) — key: pigeonhole.** The deck has 8 distinct cards; seeing 7 forces the missing one, hence the full deck, hence the class. Reconstruction here owes nothing to group theory.

- **Trace secrecy (`pgl27_coalition_trace_secrecy`) — key: a player's whole transcript is just its card.** The formal protocol run gives a corrupted player nothing beyond the card it was dealt (`pgl27_player_trace_full`), so secrecy of the executed traces reduces to the ≤3-card privacy above. This layer exists to prove the *protocol* leaks no more than the *scheme*.

- **Realistic shuffle (`pgl27_word_mixing`, `pgl27_word_view_indist`) — key: a 200-step random walk on 336 states is almost uniform.** Nobody can sample a uniform group element by hand; the honest dealer chains 200 random generator moves. The walk's distribution is within 2⁻⁴⁰ of uniform in variation distance, and since coalition views are a function of the shuffle, the perfect privacy above degrades by at most that much: views under the two secrets differ by ≤ 2⁻³⁹.

- Caveat stated in every file: all secrecy is about the **pre-reveal** execution; after the public reveal everyone learns the secret by design.

# Shamir's secret sharing, same template

## The objects

- **Field** = a finite field F_q with q > n (q prime or prime power; all arithmetic below is in F_q).
- **Secret** = a field element s ∈ F_q (not one bit: the whole field is the secret space).
- **Randomness** = k−1 uniform coefficients a₁, …, a_{k−1} ∈ F_q.
- **Sharing polynomial** = f(X) = s + a₁X + a₂X² + … + a_{k−1}X^{k−1}, a random polynomial of degree < k whose constant term is the secret.
- **Shares** = evaluations: player i's share is yᵢ = f(xᵢ), where x₁, …, xₙ are fixed, public, distinct, nonzero points.
- **Parameters** = (k, n): n players, reconstruction threshold k.

## The roles

- **Dealer**: knows s. Draws the k−1 random coefficients, forms f, evaluates it at the n public points, sends yᵢ privately to player i. All the randomness lives here (like `w0` in the PGL(2,7) run).
- **n Players**: each holds one share (xᵢ, yᵢ). Purely passive: store, then contribute when asked. Same "player is a lookup, not an executor" shape as the eight-card players.
- **Combiner** (any subset of ≥ k players, or a verifier they hand shares to): pools k shares and interpolates.

## The steps

1. Setup: publish the field F_q and the evaluation points x₁, …, xₙ.
2. Dealer encodes: picks a₁, …, a_{k−1} uniformly, sets f(X) = s + Σ aⱼXʲ.
3. Deal: player i receives yᵢ = f(xᵢ) over a private channel.
4. Reconstruction: any k players reveal their shares to the combiner.
5. Interpolate: the combiner computes the unique degree-< k polynomial through the k points and reads off the secret at zero:
   s = f(0) = Σᵢ λᵢ · yᵢ, with public Lagrange coefficients λᵢ = Πⱼ≠ᵢ xⱼ/(xⱼ − xᵢ).
   Reconstruction is a fixed public linear combination of the shares; no secrets are needed to run it.

## What "security threshold" means here

Shamir is a **perfect (k, n) threshold scheme**. The ladder collapses to a single step:

- **≤ k−1 shares: perfectly private.** For any k−1 shares and *any* candidate secret s′, there is exactly one degree-< k polynomial with constant term s′ passing through those shares. So the coalition's view has exactly the same distribution whatever the secret; zero information, with no computational assumption.
- **k shares: fully determined.** A degree-< k polynomial is uniquely fixed by k points.
- **No ramp.** Privacy at k−1 and reconstruction at k are adjacent; there is no "leaky but ambiguous" zone. This is the sharpest possible threshold behavior.

## Theorem map (classical results, stated in the repo's vocabulary)

- **Correctness** (= `ts_correct`): Lagrange interpolation. k points determine a unique polynomial of degree < k, and evaluating it at 0 returns the dealt secret. Any k-subset works, and the answers agree.

- **Privacy** (= `ts_private`) — key: every secret explains your view in exactly one way. Take any k−1 shares a coalition holds. Pick *any* candidate secret s′. Those k−1 points plus the point (0, s′) are k points, and k points fit exactly one polynomial of degree < k. So each of the q possible secrets is consistent with the coalition's view via exactly one polynomial: the counts are all 1, hence the view is equally likely under every secret. Equal counts is the whole proof.

- **Perfect secrecy, entropy form**: H(S | any k−1 shares) = H(S) for every prior on S. The counting bijection above makes the conditional distribution equal the prior. This is the analogue of `pgl27_coalition_trace_secrecy`, but exact at k−1 rather than at a threshold far below reconstruction.

- **Sharpness**: trivial here; the k-th share flips the coalition from zero information to full determination. The analogue of the `pgl27_view_dep_k4` / `pgl27_seven_reveal_class` pair, with the gap closed to zero.

- **Coding view** (McEliece and Sarwate, 1981): the share vector (f(x₁), …, f(xₙ)) is a Reed–Solomon codeword. Missing shares are erasures; with n ≥ k + 2t, reconstruction even tolerates t maliciously wrong shares by RS error correction. This is the "recovery side" story that in the eight-card scheme is played by the distinct-cards constraint at seven reveals.

- **Linearity** (basis of BGW-style multiparty computation): shares of s and s′ added coordinate-wise are shares of s + s′ under the sum polynomial; scalar multiples likewise. The eight-card scheme has no analogue; its reconstruction is a nonlinear orbit classifier.

## How Shamir would fill the same `ThresholdScheme` record

- `secretT` = F_q, `shareT` = F_q (versus `bool` and `'I_8`).
- `ts_T'` = n−1: n shares.
- `ts_k'` = k−1: coalitions strictly below k learn nothing.
- `ts_valid s shares` = "there exists a polynomial f of degree < k with f(0) = s and sharesᵢ = f(xᵢ)".
- `ts_recon` = the Lagrange linear combination through the first k coordinates.
- `ts_encode` = evaluation of the polynomial with one fixed choice of coefficients (the record's encoder is deterministic; the randomness that gives privacy lives in the validity predicate covering *all* polynomials, exactly as `orbit_valid` covers all decks of a class, not just `orbit_encode`'s representative).

## The structural contrast with the PGL(2,7) instance

- **Source of privacy**: Shamir gets it from polynomial interpolation (an algebraic counting argument, any k works). The eight-card scheme gets it from sharp 3-transitivity of the shuffle group, and group theory caps how far that can go: no finite permutation group other than Sₙ and Aₙ is more than 5-transitive, so the group-action route cannot reach arbitrary thresholds the way choosing k does.
- **Threshold shape**: perfect (k−1 private, k reconstructs) versus ramp (3 private, leaky from 4, determined at 7).
- **Secret size**: a full field element versus one bit (an orbit class).
- **Dealer randomness**: random polynomial coefficients versus a random group element (or 200-letter word) applied to a canonical deck.

# The one-line comparison this exposes

- Shamir: one mechanism (interpolation) gives correctness at k and privacy at k−1, adjacent by construction.
- PGL(2,7): *two unrelated mechanisms.* Group transitivity gives privacy (capped at 3, because 4 points have an invariant); pigeonhole on distinct cards gives recovery (at 7). Because the mechanisms are different, the thresholds don't meet, and the gap 4–6 is a ramp where the view leaks information without determining the secret.

# Concrete examples: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 cards seen

All numbers below are verified: a Python script mirroring the Rocq definitions
(generator tables, cross-ratio mod 7, the two encoder decks, the 336-element
group) reproduces exactly the collision counts proven in
`pgl27_leakage_census.v` (336, 96, 72, 36, 12, 0).

## Setup, once

- Cards are numbered 0–7. Cards 0,1,2,3 are the **hearts**.
- The secret is decided by **where the four hearts end up sitting**: take the four seat numbers that hold hearts, compute one number from them (the cross-ratio mod 7); if it is 3 or 5 the secret is `true`, otherwise `false`.
- The dealer starts from one of two fixed reference decks and applies one of the 336 allowed shuffles, all equally likely:
  - secret `false` → reference deck `0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7` (seat i holds card i),
  - secret `true` → the same deck with **cards 3 and 4 swapped**: `0,1,2,4,3,5,6,7`.
- A coalition of k seats sees its k cards. A view is **harmless** if it arises equally often under both secrets, and **conclusive** if only one secret can produce it.

## 3 cards seen

- Example: seats 0,1,2 see cards (4, 0, 7).
- Fact: *every* possible 3-card view arises exactly once under secret `false` and exactly once under secret `true`. All 336 views are shared.
- Reason: the shuffle group can move any 3 cards to any 3 seats, in exactly one way.
- Outcome: whatever you see, it's a coin flip. Chance of learning the secret: **0/336 = 0%**. This is the privacy guarantee.

## 4 cards seen

- Leakage starts, and it depends on *which* seats the coalition holds (seat sets split into two families of 42 and 28):
  - Seats {0,1,2,3}: 96 of the 336 views are harmless, 240 are conclusive → learn the secret with probability **240/336 ≈ 71%**, otherwise learn nothing.
  - Seats {0,1,2,4}: 72 harmless, 264 conclusive → **≈ 79%**.
- Concrete conclusive view: seats 0,1,2,3 see (0,1,2,3). Only the `false` deal can show this → secret is `false`. Seeing (0,1,2,4) → secret is `true`.
- Concrete harmless view: seats 0,1,2,3 see (0,1,5,6). Both secrets produce this view equally often → nothing learned.
- Why 4 is the breaking point: the group can place any 3 cards anywhere, but a 4-card pattern already carries a fingerprint of the deal — 4 points is exactly where the cross-ratio lives.

## 5 cards seen

- 36 of 336 views harmless → conclusive with probability **300/336 ≈ 89%**.
- But never *guaranteed*: for every choice of 5 seats there are still views consistent with both secrets, so a 5-seat coalition can get unlucky and learn nothing.

## 6 cards seen

- 12 of 336 views harmless → conclusive with probability **324/336 ≈ 96%**.
- The remaining 4% is very concrete. Example: seats {0,1,2,5,6,7} see cards (0,1,2,5,6,7). Then the two unseen cards must be 3 and 4, sitting at seats 3 and 4 — but in which order?
  - order (3, 4) → the deck is `0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7` → secret `false`;
  - order (4, 3) → the deck is `0,1,2,4,3,5,6,7` → secret `true`.
  One unknown swap of two cards flips the secret.
- And this is structural, not luck of this example: for *any* two hidden seats such an ambiguous view exists. So no 6-seat coalition is ever guaranteed the secret.

## 7 cards seen

- Counting does the job: 8 distinct cards, 7 seen → the missing card and its seat are forced → the whole deck is known → compute the secret.
- Conclusive with probability **336/336 = 100%**, for any choice of 7 seats. Zero ambiguous views.

## Summary

- 3 seats: learn nothing, guaranteed (0%).
- 4 seats: usually learn everything (71% or 79% depending on seats), otherwise nothing.
- 5 seats: ≈ 89% learn everything, never guaranteed.
- 6 seats: ≈ 96% learn everything, never guaranteed — the failure case is "two hidden cards, unknown order, and the order is the secret".
- 7 seats: learn everything, guaranteed.

Two different notions are being graded here: the **percentages** are averages over the dealer's random shuffle (proven as exact counts in `pgl27_leakage_census.v`, for the implemented dealer); the **guarantees** ("never" at ≤6, "always" at 7) are worst-case statements over all valid decks (`pgl27_reveal_ambiguous`, `pgl27_seven_reveal_class`). The privacy threshold 3 is the largest size where the guarantee is "nothing, ever"; 7 is the smallest where it is "everything, always"; everything between is a gamble.

# Two group elements gA, gB: when case analysis is needed, and when not

The decision rule: **it depends on whether the property respects the group
operations.** (Mechanism confirmed against `pgl27_orbit.v:243-304` and
`transitivity_privacy.v:288`.)

## When you never case-analyze (the subgroup trick)

Look at how `orbit_class_invariant` is actually proved (`pgl27_orbit.v:243-304`):

- Define `stabp` := the set of *all* permutations that preserve the classifier — the property packaged as a set.
- `group_set_stabp`: prove this set is a **group** — contains 1, closed under composition and inverse. This is pure algebra: "if gA preserves the class and gB preserves the class, then gA∘gB preserves the class" is one rewrite chain, valid for *abstract* gA, gB. No inspection of what they are.
- `gens_sub_stabp`: check the property concretely for exactly **three** elements — the generators tr, sc, inv.
- `G_sub_stabp`: conclude by `gen_subG` — the generated group is contained in any subgroup containing the generators.

Now take your arbitrary gA, gB ∈ PGL(2,7). Why no case analysis on either, let alone both:

- Every element *is* a word in the three generators. A property that survives each letter and survives composition survives every word — that's what `gen_subG` packages.
- So "shuffling by gA and then gB doesn't move the secret" is just: gA·gB ∈ G ⊆ stabp. The 336 × 336 = 112,896 possible pairs collapse to **3 concrete checks + group axioms**.
- The same shape handles `deck_stable` (distinctness survives any permutation — there it's even free, `perm_uniq`, no generator checks needed).

## When two variables collapse to one (the coset trick)

Some statements genuinely mention gA and gB together, but only through their *difference*:

- "Do the deals shuffled by gA and by gB show the same cards to coalition C?" depends only on the single element gA⁻¹·gB (does it fix the relevant points). Pair question → one-variable question → technique above or transitivity.
- Fiber counting (`rho_tuple_fiber_card`, `transitivity_privacy.v:288`) is this at scale: gA and gB produce the same ≤3-view iff they lie in the same coset of a stabilizer, and the bijection h ↦ gA·h shows all cosets have the same size. That single lemma is the entire "every view is equally likely" argument — no element ever inspected.

## When you *do* have to touch all 336 (and why not as cases)

Case analysis becomes unavoidable exactly when the property is **not closed under composition** — knowing it for gA and gB tells you nothing about gA·gB:

- Counting facts: "exactly 96 shuffles collide on this 4-view". The collision count of a product isn't a function of the factors' counts. No algebraic leverage exists; you must visit every element.
- Sporadic ground facts: "this generator word reaches this triple", "the 200-letter walk is within 2⁻⁴⁰ of uniform".
- Even then it's never 336 proof-script cases: the elements are tabulated as nat sequences and the whole quantification is one decidable boolean discharged by a single `vm_compute`.

## The litmus test, applied to gA, gB

1. Is the property true of the three generators and preserved by ∘ and ⁻¹? → subgroup trick; gA, gB, gA·gB all covered for free.
2. Does the joint statement factor through gA⁻¹·gB? → collapse to one variable, then apply test 1 or transitivity.
3. Neither? → it's a counting fact; tabulate and let the kernel evaluate it once.

There's also a practical enforcement: MathComp `{perm 'I_8}` values don't reduce under `vm_compute`, and there is no tractable 336-way `case` on an abstract group element. So the formalization couldn't brute-force pairs even if it wanted to — every proof is forced through either the algebra or the tables.
